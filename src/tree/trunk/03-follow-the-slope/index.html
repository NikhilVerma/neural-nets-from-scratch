Follow the slope

The problem that brought us here

Two test-runs per knob, every step. Fine for 2 knobs — deadly for thousands. Can we know which way to nudge without trying?

Last lesson we found which way was downhill by walking a little way in each direction and looking. It works, and it is the kind of thing you do when the thing you're measuring is a mystery — a black box you can only poke.

But the score isn't a mystery. We wrote it. Every step of it is written down: take the input, multiply by the multiplier knob, add the add-on knob, subtract what the answer should have been, square it, average. There is no hidden machinery in there. So why are we probing it like a stranger?

The simplest thing that could work

Here's the question worth asking, and it's a question about the formula rather than about the machine: if I turned the multiplier knob by a hair, how much would the score move? Not "would it go up or down" — how much, and which way, per unit of turn. Call that number the slope of the score along that knob.

If you know the slope you know everything the two test-runs would have told you and more. A negative slope means turning that knob up brings the score down. A big slope means this knob matters a lot right now; a slope near zero means this knob has stopped mattering and you should leave it alone. The up-and-down probing was only ever a clumsy way of measuring the slope by hand.

And you can get it from the formula directly. Follow one example through: the machine's answer is the multiplier times the input, plus the add-on. Turn the multiplier up by a hair and the answer moves by the input — a hair times the input, to be exact. Turn the add-on up by a hair and the answer moves by exactly that hair. The score is the mistake squared, and squaring means the score moves by twice the mistake for every unit the answer moves. Put those together and you have both slopes, for one example, in one line of arithmetic each. Average them over all sixty examples and you have the slope of the whole score.

Then the update is almost too small to notice: move each knob a little bit against its slope. Uphill is where the slope points, so we go the other way. How far is "a little bit" is one number we pick, the same for every knob — too big and the machine leaps over the bottom of the valley and bounces out; too small and it inches.

Count what this costs. One walk through the examples gets us the slope for every knob at once, because each example contributes its bit to both slopes as it goes past. Not two runs per knob. One run, total, no matter how many knobs. The machine never has to try anything to find out which way to go.

Watch it learn

Left: the examples and the machine's current line. Right: the score after each pass through the data. The readouts show both knobs and the slope currently pulling on each one — watch the slopes shrink toward zero as the line settles. That's the machine arriving, not stopping.

On the straight-line data this reaches the same answer as last lesson's nudging for roughly half the work — and that ratio is the best case, because two knobs is where nudging is cheapest. At a thousand knobs, nudging costs two thousand runs a step and this still costs one.

Watch it break

Press Data: Line ↔ Curve above. The examples now come from a rule that bends — a gentle U. Train it as long as you like.

The score falls, flattens out, and stops, a long way above zero. Nothing is broken: the slopes really do reach zero, the machine really is at the bottom of its valley, and if you press for another ten thousand passes not one digit will change. It has found the best straight line through curved data, exactly as asked. The best straight line through curved data is simply terrible.

This is a different kind of failure from the previous two, and it's worth being precise about the difference. Lesson 1 was too slow. Lesson 2 was too expensive. Both of those are complaints about the search — better search, better result. Here the search is flawless and the answer is still wrong, because the answer we want is not in the set of things this machine can say. Multiply and add is a straight line. It can be steeper, it can slide up and down, and that is the whole of its vocabulary. You cannot search your way to a shape you cannot express.

What engineers call this
w and b
The two knobs, in every codebase you'll ever read: w for weight (our multiplier) and b for bias (our add-on). The code in this folder switches to those names here.
derivative
The slope — how much one number moves when another moves by a hair.
gradient
All the slopes at once, one per knob, collected into a list.
gradient descent
The whole method on this page: work out the slopes, step against them, repeat.
learning rate
How far we step per unit of slope. The single most fiddled-with number in the field.
epoch
One pass through the whole set of examples — one press of the button.
analytic vs numerical gradients
Reading the slope off the formula (this lesson) versus measuring it by poking (last lesson). Both give the same answer; only one is affordable.
underfitting / model capacity
What the curve demo shows: the machine is not wrong because it trained badly, it's wrong because it cannot represent the answer.
