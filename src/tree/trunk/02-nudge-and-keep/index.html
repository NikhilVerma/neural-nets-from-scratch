Nudge and keep

The problem that brought us here

Random guessing never settles — can we guess smarter instead of more?

Guessing at random got us into the right neighbourhood in about a hundred tries and then spent twenty thousand more shuffling around in it. The reason is that every guess starts from nothing. The machine finds a good setting, learns nothing from it, and throws the next pair of dice from exactly the same place it threw the first.

So here is the smaller, better question. Forget finding the answer in one lucky roll. We're standing at some setting already. Which way is downhill from here?

The simplest thing that could work

Ask. Take the multiplier knob and turn it up a little — score that. Turn it down a little instead — score that. Now we have three numbers: where we are, a little up, a little down. Keep whichever of the three is lowest. Then do the same for the add-on knob. That's one step, and every step either improves the score or leaves it alone. It can never get worse, because we only move when moving helps.

One thing is missing: how far is "a little"? Start with a nudge of 1. When a step comes back with neither knob having a better direction, that doesn't mean we've arrived — it means we've arrived as far as a nudge this big can tell. So halve the nudge and look again at finer resolution. Big strides while we're far away, tiny ones as we close in, with nothing deciding the schedule but the machine's own failure to improve.

It works. It genuinely settles on an answer and stays there, which is more than the last lesson could say. But notice what we paid, because it is the whole story of the next lesson: to move one knob one step, we had to run the machine over every example, twice — once for up, once for down. Two test-runs per knob, every single step, and none of that work is the actual learning. It's just asking for directions.

Watch it break

Nothing breaks on this page — that's the trouble. It converges, it's stable, and with two knobs it costs four test-runs a step, which is nothing. The failure only shows up when you ask what happens to a machine that isn't a toy. Ours has two knobs. Drag the slider and price the same training run on a machine with more.

Ten thousand knobs is still a small machine by any modern standard, and it already wants twenty million full sweeps through the data to train for a thousand steps. Every one of those sweeps exists to answer a single yes-or-no question about a single knob: up, or down? We are running the entire machine, over the entire dataset, to extract one bit.

Which starts to feel absurd once you say it out loud. The score isn't a locked box we can only probe by trying things. It's a formula we wrote ourselves. Surely the formula knows which way is down.

What engineers call this
hill climbing
The strategy on this page: from where you stand, look only at your immediate neighbours and move to the best one. (Downhill here, since low scores win.)
coordinate descent
The detail that we move one knob at a time rather than all of them together.
finite differences
Working out which way a formula is heading by evaluating it at two nearby points and comparing — exactly our up-nudge and down-nudge.
step size / step decay
Our nudge size, and the habit of halving it when progress stops.
function evaluations
Our test-runs. In real optimisation work this is the number people count.
